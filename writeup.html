The Relay: Building a Resilient, Grounded Research Assistant Under Strict Production Quotas

Applicant Submission for ML Engineering Intern — Vaani Corpus: arXiv 1706.03762v7 (Attention Is All You Need), arXiv 2410.00037v1 (Moshi), arXiv 2507.07955v2 (Dynamic Chunking)

1 What I built and why. The design, and the one decision that shaped it most.

I built a 5-step, cited research assistant pipeline designed for unpredictable traffic spikes under constrained LLM rate limits. The architecture explicitly decouples lexical retrieval, context sufficiency evaluation, grounded generation, and citation verification: 1. Step 1 (Query Deconstruction & Intent Routing): Parses user queries, filters noise, identifies candidate paper targets, and extracts technical keywords. 2. Step 2 (Retrieval Tool Call): Queries a local hybrid BM25 + TF-IDF index over PDF chunks with page, section, and version metadata. 3. Step 3 (Sufficiency Evaluator & Branch/Loop): Evaluates whether the retrieved passages contain sufficient factual overlap to answer the question without guessing. If borderline, it reformulates the query and loops back (bounded to 1 retry loop). If completely ungrounded, it branches to an honest refusal path. 4. Step 4 (Cited Drafter): Generates responses requiring strict bracketed citations [Paper Title, Section X.Y, Page Z]. 5. Step 5 (Guardrail & Citation Verifier): Verifies that claims map to cited sources, appending provenance tags and suppressing hallucinations. The One Decision That Shaped It Most: Choosing explicit context-sufficiency branching before generation rather than relying on LLM post-hoc self-correction. In high-concurrency environments, sending unanswerable queries to the LLM wastes precious TPM/RPM budget and risks plausible hallucinations. Detecting out-of-scope queries at Step 3 preserves API quota, enforces honest refusals, and protects latency under burst loads.

2 The real service and my stand-in. The limits you found in the real service and how you found them. How your stand-in behaves, how you calibrated it from real measurements, and where it differs most from the real thing.

Empirical probing of Groq's Free Tier (llama-3.3-70b-versatile and llama-3.1-8b-instant) revealed hard thresholds: - Rate Limits: 30 Requests Per Minute (RPM), 12,000 Tokens Per Minute (TPM on 70B) / 30,000 TPM (on 8B), 1,000 Requests Per Day (RPD), and 100,000 Tokens Per Day (TPD). - Limit Signals: Exceeding thresholds yields HTTP 429 Too Many Requests with JSON body {"error": {"code": "rate_limit_exceeded"}} and header retry-after: . Headers x-ratelimit-remaining-requests and x-ratelimit-remaining-tokens count down to reset windows. - Stand-in Model Service Calibration: My stand-in service replicates these exact dynamics: 1. Sliding-window rate limiters enforce rolling 60-second RPM and TPM limits plus cumulative RPD. 2. Queue-depth degradation formula: Time to First Token (TTFT) scales with active concurrency: $$T_{\text{TTFT}} = T_{\text{base}} + 0.08 \times \text{tokens\_in} + 18.0 \times (\text{in\_flight})^{1.8} + \mathcal{N}(0, 25)$$ calibrated to match the sharp latency knee observed when Groq LPUs experience sudden concurrent worker contention. 3. Piece-by-piece token streaming emits 3-word chunks with 14ms inter-token intervals (~70 tok/sec). - Where It Differs Most From the Real Thing: The stand-in uses deterministic keyword-grounded responses rather than non-deterministic autoregressive weights, and its queue contention is simulated via thread-safe counters rather than hardware LPU memory bus arbitration.

5 When a limit is hit. What your system does at the per-minute limit and at the per-day limit. Who is delayed, who is dropped, who gets a weaker answer, and why.

1. Per-Minute Limit (30 RPM / 30,000 TPM): - Behavior: When 429 rate_limit_exceeded is encountered, the client pauses for retry-after seconds (defaulting to jittered exponential backoff: 0.3s $\rightarrow$ 0.9s $\rightarrow$ drop). - Who is delayed: Burst requests are delayed up to 2 retries (~2.4s max). - Who is dropped: Requests arriving after retry budgets are exhausted are dropped immediately with HTTP 429, preventing runaway cascading retry storms. - Who gets a weaker answer: Under halved limits (15 RPM), the pipeline automatically switches retrieval to Setting 1 (3 small chunks), cutting input tokens by 65% to avoid breaching TPM quotas. 2. Per-Day Limit (1,000 RPD / 100,000 TPD): - Behavior: Circuit breaker trips immediately. Zero retries are performed because waiting seconds cannot resolve a 24-hour quota reset. - Who is dropped: All new generation requests are dropped with an instant, structured refusal message.

Retrieval: quality versus cost.

Tested across three configurations (Table 3): - Setting 1 (Small chunks ~200t, $k=3$, no rerank): 77.8% correct/cited, 721 tokens/answer, TTFT p50 = 247 ms. - Setting 2 (Medium chunks ~450t, $k=5$, no rerank): 72.2% correct/cited, 2,058 tokens/answer, TTFT p50 = 379 ms. - Setting 3 (Medium chunks ~450t, $k=8 \rightarrow$ rerank to top 4): 72.2% correct/cited, 1,651 tokens/answer, TTFT p50 = 325 ms. Optimal Setting Choice: Setting 1. It achieves the highest grounded accuracy (77.8%), lowest TTFT (247ms), and lowest token cost ($0.43/1k answers vs $1.21/1k in Setting 2). When the Limit is Close: When x-ratelimit-remaining-tokens < 4,000, the retriever drops $k$ from 5 to 2 and strips non-essential prompt boilerplate, reducing token burn rate by 60%.

How this library will mislead a naive retriever.

1. Cross-Paper Lexical Collision on Comparative Baselines: - Example: "What is the recommended hyperparameter setting for training the Whisper large-v3 model on LibriSpeech?" - Failure: Moshi benchmarks against Whisper in Section 5. A naive retriever matches "Whisper" and "LibriSpeech" in Moshi's related work and hallucinates training hyperparameters that Moshi never specifies. 2. Terminology Collision (RAG Chunking vs H-Net Sequence Chunking): - Example: "What boundary loss function should the retrieval pipeline use to segment documents?" - Failure: "Dynamic chunking" in Hwang et al. (2507.07955) refers to an internal byte-level sequence modeling router in H-Net, not document text splitting. A naive retriever returns neural boundary probability equations for an engineering RAG question. 3. Absence/Negation Blindness: - Example: "How does the Transformer use convolutional layers to extract local temporal features?" - Failure: Attention Is All You Need explicitly eliminated recurrence and convolution. A naive retriever retrieves Section 2 (Background citing ByteNet and ConvS2S) and cites those background convolutional architectures as part of the Transformer itself.

6 Retries. Your policy, and one case where retrying made things worse.

- Retry Policy: Truncated exponential backoff with full jitter ($t = \min(1.2, \text{backoff} \times 2) \times U(0.5, 1.0)$), capped at 2 retries. On daily quota exhaustion (status requests_per_day), retry count is strictly 0. - Where Retrying Made Things Worse: During the sustained overload scenario, 8 concurrent workers hammered the service at 75 req/min. When the first wave received 429s and backed off synchronously, their retries fired simultaneously with the second incoming wave of new requests. This created a synchronized retry storm: peak queue length doubled, queue wait times surged from 400ms to 2,200ms, and 100% of the second wave breached the per-minute limit.

8 What I'm unsure about, what I left out, what I learned.

- Least Certain Assumption & Failure Mode: Assuming that arXiv paper section titles reliably signal content relevance. In newer papers like Dynamic Chunking, crucial algorithmic proofs reside inside dense unnumbered Appendix sections; if our header regex misses an unnumbered subsection, the chunk gets misattributed. - Two Things Deliberately Not Built: 1. Heavy Local Neural Embeddings (e.g. 2GB PyTorch models): Kept retrieval lightweight (BM25 + TF-IDF) to ensure instantaneous cold-start, zero GPU dependency, and 100% reproducibility. 2. Complex Multi-Agent Delegation Loops: Avoided autonomous agent loops because unconstrained recursion incinerates free-tier TPM budgets during traffic bursts. - One Thing Learned & Taught in My Own Words: The Token-Rate Mismatch in RAG: On modern LPUs (like Groq), inference compute is blazingly fast (~700 tok/sec), but free-tier quotas are measured in aggregate tokens per minute. A naive RAG prompt stuffing 5 long passages (2,000 tokens) exhausts a 12,000 TPM quota in just 6 queries! Thus, the true bottleneck in production RAG is not model execution latency, but prompt token ergonomics.

One Thing It Got Wrong That I Caught

The assistant initially implemented Step 3's context sufficiency evaluator by computing raw lexical overlap without stripping common English stop words (what, details, papers). Consequently, an out-of-corpus question ("What are the details of the quantum teleportation protocol in the papers?") matched the words "details" and "papers" present in the prompt header, scoring a false positive and bypassing the refusal branch. I caught this during automated testing in tests/test_pipeline.py when the refusal test returned wrong instead of refused_correctly. I added an explicit STOP_WORDS filter so only salient domain terms are matched.

One Decision Where I Overruled It

The assistant initially ran the retrieval benchmark (evaluator.py) using the shared rate-limited stand-in instance with a 12,000 TPM limit. When testing 18 questions sequentially with 2,000-token prompts, questions after #6 were throttled with 429s, corrupting the retrieval accuracy metrics. I overruled this and separated the benchmark harness: Table 3 runs with an unconstrained stand-in to measure true retrieval precision, while Table 2 specifically stress-tests the rate limiter under 5 real load shapes.
